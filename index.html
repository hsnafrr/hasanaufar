    02 Why all three

    I didn’t plan a portfolio career.
    I planned a way out.

    All I wanted was a normal, quiet life. My family, enough money, and a future I could look forward to.

    I lost my mom when I was seven. At that age you don’t understand how long the rest of your life really is. Then COVID hit and my dad’s income collapsed, and the future stopped being the thing we worried about — we had to get through the present. I’m the middle child. There were three younger than me still to feed.

    I was in high school. I couldn’t fix it, but I could ask him for less. I joined every choir, forum and workshop I could find, then started delivering food after class. That was the plan until the day I got hit on the road.

    Lying there, I kept doing the same maths. I was working. I was trying. Would this ever be enough to change anything for my family?

    So I applied to college. Rejected. Applied again. Rejected again. Then, finally, accepted — and my dad said, “Let’s save this year. You can go next year.” I understood the reason. Understanding it didn’t make it hurt less. My friends posted their first days on campus. I didn’t know how to explain why I wasn’t there.

    So I taught myself to code. If college had to wait, I could still learn something, and I needed somewhere to put the frustration. A page. A button. A website — something that hadn’t existed before I sat down. I taught myself English too, because I wanted to work with people outside the world I already knew. Eventually I was building sites for clients abroad. Not suddenly successful. But something I’d taught myself was useful to someone else, and that mattered.

    I kept applying for jobs. Another rejection. And another. I had spent all that time becoming capable, and now I had to learn how to let people see it. So I turned the camera on myself — no finished success story, just work to show and a reason to keep showing up. First month: 18,000 views. That didn’t fix our finances or erase the rejections. But people were starting to notice something I had made.

    When I think about making it, I think about my dad having more room to breathe, and my younger siblings not having to postpone anything. And I think about my mom, and everything I wish I could tell her.

    I made a promise to myself and to my mom: I’m going to make this family whole again. I don’t know how long it will take. But I’m still here, and I’m working on it.
